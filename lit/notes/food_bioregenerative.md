# Bioregenerative Food Systems for Space: Literature Survey and Research Gaps

## Candidate Research Gaps

### Gap 1: Crop Physiological Response to Combined Lunar Gravity (1/6g) + Reduced Atmospheric Pressure

**Name:** *Gravitational-Pressure Interaction in Plant Growth Under Lunar Conditions*

**Closest Prior Work:** Wheeler (2018, NASA/TM-2018-001979); "The Problem of Partial Gravity" (2020, NTRS/20205010170); Monje et al. (2020, NTRS/20205008157 – Artemis III concept).

**Why It Matters:** Lunar base sustainability requires bioregenerative food production, but Wheeler (2018) and the 2020 partial-gravity review both explicitly state that **plant growth under combined 1/6g + reduced pressure (6–10 kPa) has not been experimentally demonstrated**. Current ISS operations occur in microgravity at 101 kPa; lunar habitats will face simultaneous gravity and pressure reduction, creating novel physiological stress. Crew food security and O₂/CO₂ balance depend on quantifying whether crops remain viable.

**Maturity/Tractability:** This is highly tractable for ground-based validation. A parabolic-flight or short-radius centrifuge can recreate 1/6g in a controlled chamber with reduced-pressure capability (available at NASA/KSC or partner labs). A 4–8 week bench study with lettuce or radish would yield biomass, gas-exchange, and root-development data. Model prediction of yield loss could then inform mission food-production area and resupply requirements.

**Novelty / Tractability / Relevance Score:** 
- **Novelty: 9/10** — Unpublished experimental data; direct gap in published literature.  
- **Tractability: 7/10** — Requires centrifuge + pressure chamber, but hardware exists; single-crop studies are 4–8 weeks.  
- **Relevance: 9/10** — Directly critical for Artemis/lunar surface planning; NASA is actively designing Artemis III food systems (Monje et al., 2020).  
- **Overall: 25/30**

### Gap 2: Real-Time Nutrient-Drift Detection and Control Under Altered Gravity

**Name:** *Closed-Loop Nutrient Cycling and Ion-Exchange Management in Hydroponic Systems Under Partial Gravity*

**Closest Prior Work:** Wheeler (2014, NASA/TP-2014-017323) notes hydroponic design risks under altered gravity; Unleashing APMBR (2024, NASA/TM-2024-007366) describes wastewater-recovery bioreactors but not gravity-dependent ion drift; Hydroponic Recycling (1998, NASA/TM-1998-007456) demonstrates ground-based nutrient reuse.

**Why It Matters:** Modern ISS plant systems (Veggie, APH) rely on hydroponic/capillary delivery. Closed-loop bioregenerative food production requires stable nutrient-ion ratios (N:K:Ca:Mg, etc.) to avoid deficiency-induced yield loss. Under reduced gravity, convective mixing, settling, and ionic drift behave differently than on Earth; the 2022 Frontiers paper notes long-term nutrient-solution drift as a risk requiring "new control strategies." **No published sensors or control algorithms exist for detecting K⁺, Ca²⁺, NO₃⁻ drift in real time aboard spacecraft under reduced gravity.**

**Maturity/Tractability:** Highly tractable modeling and bench-test contribution. A graduate-level project could: (1) develop ion-selective electrode (ISE) or optical absorption spectroscopy protocols for plant-growth chambers; (2) run 30-day hydroponic lettuce trials under 1g (control) and reduced gravity (parabolic flight, 15–20 sec intervals); (3) collect time-series ion concentration and yield data; (4) fit a drift model (e.g., ion precipitation, microbial uptake variation) and propose a closed-loop feedback control law. Validation on ISS APH would follow.

**Novelty / Tractability / Relevance Score:**
- **Novelty: 8/10** — Sensors exist; integration + gravity dependence is novel.  
- **Tractability: 8/10** — Parabolic flight feasible; simple ion analysis; moderate data volume.  
- **Relevance: 8/10** — Essential for any long-duration closed-loop ECLSS; ISS (APH, future Lunar Halo) are primary users.  
- **Overall: 24/30**

### Gap 3: Seed Production and Multigenerational Crop Viability Under Microgravity and Partial Gravity

**Name:** *Reproductive Development, Seed Yield, and Germination Rates in Reduced Gravity Across Multiple Generations*

**Closest Prior Work:** APH (2016, NTRS) is designed for "multigenerational studies" but no published data on seed production/viability in microgravity; Interactions Between Gravitropism and Phototropism (2004, NTRS/20040088310) documents altered tropism in reduced gravity; Proximate Composition of Soybean (1990) measured yield at 1g.

**Why It Matters:** **Bioregenerative food systems assume continuous crop cycles, implying seed production in situ.** Early-stage ECLSS research (Wheeler 2018) notes that wheat, soybean, and potato seed production has not been validated in microgravity. For moon/Mars missions, importing seed every resupply is mass-prohibitive; crews must produce seed on-site. If reduced gravity (1/6g or 0.38g) disrupts pollen tube growth, embryo development, or seed dormancy, crop cycles collapse. This is a **single point of failure** not quantified in the literature.

**Maturity/Tractability:** Tractable but longer timeline. A ground-based study using APH (accessible to selected researchers via NASA partnerships) could grow lettuce, radish, or soybean from seed → flowering → seed maturity under controlled microgravity simulation (parabolic flights, 3–4 campaigns for full cycles). Measure pollen viability, silique/pod development, seed mass, and germination rate. Alternative: request APH ISS allocation for seed-production experiment (135-day capability supports full cycle for radish, lettuce).

**Novelty / Tractability / Relevance Score:**
- **Novelty: 9/10** — No published seed-production data in microgravity for major bioregenerative crops.  
- **Tractability: 6/10** — Requires 6–12 months of ISS time or multiple parabolic campaigns; long biological cycle.  
- **Relevance: 9/10** — Makes or breaks long-duration food autonomy for moon/Mars; critical for mission feasibility studies.  
- **Overall: 24/30**

### Gap 4: Mars-Gravity (0.38g) Crop Physiology and Yield Projections

**Name:** *Plant Growth, Photosynthesis, and Yield Under Martian Partial Gravity (0.38g) Across Multiple Crop Species*

**Closest Prior Work:** Partial Gravity Problem (2020, NTRS/20205010170) identifies 0.38g as insufficient for human musculoskeletal conditioning but **does not address crops**; Wheeler reviews candidate crops for bioregenerative systems (wheat, soybean, lettuce, potato) but assumes 1g design baselines; lunar (1/6g) is well-cited, Mars rarely so.

**Why It Matters:** Mars exploration plans (NASA HRP, ESA) assume multi-year surface missions (2+ years at 0.38g). **No peer-reviewed experimental data quantifies whether wheat, soybean, lettuce, or potato yields are comparable to Earth under 0.38g + reduced pressure + altered soil/water physics.** Current bioregenerative system models (Wheeler, 2018; Monje, 2020) are validated at 1g only. Yield loss of even 20–30% under Mars gravity would require significantly larger growth area, increasing mission mass and power.

**Maturity/Tractability:** Moderately tractable via centrifuge. A series of short-radius centrifuge (radius ~1–2 m, ~40 rpm) experiments can sustain 0.38g continuously for 30–60 days. Run parallel trials with lettuce, radish (fast-cycling) and wheat (longer cycle, possibly multi-week) under controlled 0.38g. Measure canopy photosynthesis, evapotranspiration, dry-matter accumulation, harvest index. Model yield vs. gravity relation; extrapolate to full mission crop schedule. Moderate resource requirement but significant data value.

**Novelty / Tractability / Relevance Score:**
- **Novelty: 9/10** — Mars gravity crop data essentially absent from published literature.  
- **Tractability: 7/10** — Centrifuge infrastructure needed; 30–60 day studies feasible; data collection straightforward.  
- **Relevance: 8/10** — Essential for Mars mission feasibility assessment; less urgent than lunar (Artemis timeline nearer).  
- **Overall: 24/30**

### Gap 5: Integrated Bioregenerative System Modeling (Plants + Algae + Microbial Recyclers) Under Reduced Gravity

**Name:** *Dynamic Closed-Loop System Model for Coupled Plant, Algal, and Microbial ECLSS Under Variable Gravity Conditions*

**Closest Prior Work:** Enhancing Control Systems (2022, Frontiers) develops metabolic + energy models for lettuce alone; Energy Cascade Models (2023, in silico Plants) predict biomass and transpiration; APMBR (2024, NASA/TM-2024-007366) demonstrates microbial wastewater treatment; Coupling Plant Growth and Waste (1992) integrates plant growth + decomposition at 1g baseline.

**Why It Matters:** **True bioregenerative food systems must couple plant production (food, O₂), algal/microbial CO₂ scrubbing/N-fixation (air revitalization), and bacterial decomposition (waste recycling).** Wheeler (2018) and Plant and Microbial Science (2023) both emphasize that integration is immature. No published model simultaneously predicts O₂ balance, nutrient recycling efficiency, and biomass yield when all three modules interact under variable gravity. Without such integration, crew food security and air quality cannot be co-designed; mission ECLSS remains over-sized or risky.

**Maturity/Tractability:** Moderately tractable for a systems modeling contribution. Collect published kinetic parameters for plant photosynthesis/respiration (Wheeler, 2018; RUE literature), algal growth (cyanobacteria in closed-loop reactors), and microbial decomposition (APMBR, 2024). Build a compartmental model (plant biomass, nutrient pool, algal stock, microbial biomass, CO₂ partial pressure, O₂ partial pressure). Calibrate and validate against ground-based Bio-Plex or Earth-based bioregenerative testbeds (BIOS-3, historical data). Extend with gravity-dependent parameters (sedimentation, convection, ion drift from Gap 2). Output: prediction of crew-support capacity (days on-site) vs. habitat size and gravity. Does not require spaceflight; bench validation is sufficient.

**Novelty / Tractability / Relevance Score:**
- **Novelty: 7/10** — Component models exist; integration and gravity dependence are novel but incremental on Wheeler/Monje.  
- **Tractability: 8/10** — Computer modeling only; parameters from published sources; no experiments required (though field data refinement could follow).  
- **Relevance: 8/10** — High for NASA/ESA mission planning; enables optimization of habitat layout and crop area.  
- **Overall: 23/30**

### Gap 6: Optimal Lighting Spectrum and Photoperiod for Bioregenerative Crops Under Realistic Power Constraints

**Name:** *LED Spectrum Optimization for Maximal Radiation-Use Efficiency and Nutritional Yield in Space-Constrained Food Production Systems*

**Closest Prior Work:** ISS Veggie trials (VEG-04, 2019) tested different LED spectra for mustard; NASA Vertical Farming (2020) publishes RUE values for wheat (0.59), soybean (0.43), lettuce (0.46) g/mol photons, but spectrum dependency not clearly separated; Ground studies (Wheeler et al.) use fixed LED types.

**Why It Matters:** LED power scales with habitat mass and resupply logistics. Current ISS systems (Veggie, APH) use fixed red/blue/white blends; optimal spectrum may differ under microgravity (altered starch distribution, gravitropism loss) vs. partial gravity. **Published data conflate spectrum effects with gravity effects; no systematic LED-spectrum optimization for space crops under reduced gravity exists.** A 10–20% RUE improvement via spectrum tuning directly reduces habitat grow-area and power demand for a given crew size, high leverage for mission feasibility.

**Maturity/Tractability:** Highly tractable bench study. Grow lettuce under 4–6 LED spectra variants (e.g., red:blue ratio 2:1, 3:1, 4:1; white + red combination) in identical environmental chambers. Measure photosynthetic rate (LI-COR, fluorescence), dry-matter accumulation, leaf nutrient content, and sensory quality. Run at 1g (terrestrial baseline); optionally repeat under parabolic-flight reduced gravity for one spectrum to isolate gravity × spectrum interaction. Output: RUE, edible yield, and nutrient bioavailability ranking. Simple experiment; high practical impact. Could integrate into APH ISS operations.

**Novelty / Tractability / Relevance Score:**
- **Novelty: 6/10** — Spectrum effects well-studied on Earth; space-gravity dependence is gap, but marginal novelty.  
- **Tractability: 9/10** — Straightforward, 8–12 week ground study; low risk; uses available equipment.  
- **Relevance: 7/10** — Practical for mission design but not a blocking risk like multigenerational or gravity physiology gaps.  
- **Overall: 22/30**

## Summary

Six candidate gaps span fundamental science (gravity physiology, multigenerational seed production), engineering (nutrient control, LED optimization), and systems modeling (integrated ECLSS). The highest-priority gaps—**1 (lunar gravity + pressure), 2 (nutrient control), 3 (seed viability)**—are experimentally tractable, have clear prior work anchors, and directly enable mission designs for Artemis (moon) and eventual Mars surface habitats. Gaps 4, 5, and 6 extend the scope to Mars and systems-level optimization.
