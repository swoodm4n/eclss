# ECLSS Waste Management: Literature Review & Research Gaps

## Sources

### Primary NASA Technical Reports

1. **Development of a Universal Waste Management System** — NASA Technical Reports Server (NTRS), Citation #20130011635
   - Recent NASA reconceptualization of waste collection system (WCS) addressing hardware volume/weight constraints. Describes UWMS design incorporating favorable features from Space Shuttle and ISS systems. Key finding: WCS is central component within ECLSS hardware suite.

2. **Waste Management Options for Long-Duration Space Missions: When to Reject, Reuse, or Recycle** — NASA NTRS, Citation #20140010284
   - Core reference for long-duration mission strategy. Documents challenge that ~2,600 kg waste/year for 4-person crew on 1-year missions requires strategic choices: disposal via airlock, processing into useful gases for propulsion, or recycling. Quantifies waste generation and evaluates multiple pathways.

3. **Trash-to-Gas: Using Waste Products to Minimize Logistical Mass During Long Duration Space Missions** — NASA NTRS, Citation #20140002806
   - Describes NASA Orbital Syngas/Commodity Augmentation Reactor (OSCAR) for thermal conversion of waste to methane, water, and raw material feedstocks. Demonstrates microgravity-relevant waste-to-commodity technology under development.

4. **Human Factor Investigation of Waste Processing System During the HI-SEAS 4 Month Mars Analog Mission** — NASA NTRS, Citation #20140017443
   - Ground-based Mars analog (HI-SEAS) data on crew interaction with Trash-to-Gas system. Provides operational insights from ~120-day isolation study relevant to human factors in waste processing.

### Heat Melt Compactor (HMC) Series

5. **Water Recovery with the Heat Melt Compactor in a Microgravity Environment** — NASA NTRS, Citation #20150019482
   - Key technology development report. HMC extracts >90% moisture from crew waste, compacts to solid pucks, and melts plastic for encapsulation. Critical for dual-function waste processing (volume reduction + water recovery). Water reuse essential for reducing resupply mass.

6. **Analysis of Water Recovery Rate from the Heat Melt Compactor** — NASA NTRS, Citation #20140011480
   - Quantitative analysis of HMC water extraction efficiency, informing reliability predictions for life support closure.

7. **Gas Effluent Analysis of the Heat Melt Compactor** — NASA NTRS, Citation #20205004359
   - Characterizes off-gas composition during HMC operation, safety-relevant for closed spacecraft environments.

8. **Characterization of Heat Melt Compactor (HMC) Product Water** — NASA NTRS, Citation #20140008312
   - Chemical and microbial quality of recovered HMC water; critical for potability and system integration.

9. **Heat Melt Compactor Development Progress** — NASA NTRS, Citation #20170011317
   - Multi-year development summary; tracks maturation of HMC toward flight readiness.

10. **Space Mission Utility and Requirements for a Heat Melt Compactor** — NASA NTRS, Citation #20160008947
    - Mission-level requirement definition; justifies HMC utility for deep space missions.

### Alternative Compaction Technologies

11. **The Trash Compaction Processing System (TCPS)** — NASA NTRS (ICES-2023 paper), Citation #20220017801
    - Assesses complexities of trash loading/unloading in microgravity. Intended to reduce volume, ensure safe disposal, and maintain storable form. Highlights crew usability challenges in zero-G.

12. **Development of a Mechanical Trash Compactor (Mpactor)** — NASA NTRS (ICES-2025 draft), Citation #20250001789
    - Pneumatically driven compactor for CEV/exploration missions. Manages food packaging, sanitary wipes, clothing, tape. Recent development at NASA Ames for Constellation Program.

13. **The design and fabrication of a prototype trash compacting unit** — NASA NTRS, Citation #19740017524
    - Early trash compactor design; historical reference showing decades of compaction focus.

16. **Proposed Objective Odor Control Test Methodology for Waste Containment** — NASA NTRS, Citation #20140005959
    - Framework for standardized odor testing of waste disposal bags for Orion CEV. Establishes methodology for breakthrough time measurement.

17. **Odor Control Test Plan of the Urine Containment Bag (UCB) for Orion Utilization** — NASA NTRS, Citation #20150018580
    - Specific test protocol to evaluate odor containment of UCB in spacecraft-simulated environment; goal to determine breakthrough time and acceptability threshold.

18. **Odor Control Test Report of the Urine Containment Bag** — NASA NTRS, Citation #20150012215
    - Results of UCB odor control testing; provides quantitative breakthrough data.

## Candidate Research Gaps

### Gap 1: Quantitative Microbial Dynamics Modeling for Waste Decomposition in Variable Gravity

**Closest Prior Work**: Microbiomes of "Lunar Palace 365" (Ref. 26); Microbial characterization papers (Ref. 30); Urine pretreatment chemistry (Ref. 15)

**Why It Matters**: Current waste processing strategies assume microbial stabilization (Ref. 14) or chemical pretreatment (Ref. 15), but Mars (0.38g) and lunar (0.17g) missions will operate in partial gravity—not microgravity or 1g. Microbial growth rates, oxygen diffusion, and metabolite accumulation have not been characterized under Martian or lunar gravity. Predicting odor breakthrough, sterilization times, and microbial population control is essential.

**Tractability**: Bench tests feasible via centrifuge experiments with waste simulant + indigenous microbes under controlled partial gravity. Could validate ODEs for population dynamics. Modeling framework could be hobbyist-compatible (Python + differential equations).

**Scores**:
- **Novelty: 8/10** — No published microbial kinetic models for partial gravity waste decomposition; gravity effects on microbial ecology are an open frontier.
- **Tractability: 7/10** — Standard microbiology + centrifuge access + numerical modeling; feasible for graduate research or small team.
- **Relevance: 9/10** — Mars/Moon missions are baseline NASA planning. Odor and sterilization safety are crew-critical. This directly informs waste management strategy selection.

### Gap 2: Odor Breakthrough Prediction as a Function of Waste Compaction State

**Closest Prior Work**: Odor control test plans (Refs. 16, 17, 18); Heat Melt Compactor products (Refs. 5–8); TCPS microgravity usability studies (Ref. 11)

**Why It Matters**: Odor tests (Ref. 18) measure breakthrough time for full bags, but compacted waste (HMC, mechanical compactor) is a solid or semi-solid with changing surface area, porosity, and sorptive properties. As waste is compressed, odor headspace changes, but no model predicts breakthrough vs. compaction state. Crew needs predictive timeline for containment bag replacement—critical for long missions where resupply is limited.

**Tractability**: Bench test with compaction rig + odor sensor array over time; generate empirical curves for different waste types. Develop simple model (e.g., Fickian diffusion + sorption isotherm) validated against data. Achievable with commercial sensors and standard equipment.

**Scores**:
- **Novelty: 7/10** — Odor testing is established (Ref. 16), but compaction-coupled breakthrough modeling is not found in literature.
- **Tractability: 8/10** — Straightforward experimental design; existing odor sensing technology; model complexity is moderate.
- **Relevance: 8/10** — Affects crew quarters habitability and waste system design. Especially important for Moon base, where resupply is infrequent.

### Gap 3: Particle Size, Density, and Settling Behavior Under Partial Gravity During Compaction

**Closest Prior Work**: TCPS loading/unloading studies in microgravity (Ref. 11); Mpactor design for micro-G (Ref. 12); Microgravity waste conversion experiments (Ref. 30)

**Why It Matters**: Compactors (heat-melt, mechanical) are designed and tested for 0g, where particles suspend uniformly. Under partial gravity (Mars or Moon), settling, density segregation, and particle jamming will occur during compression. Performance, efficiency, and safety (e.g., jamming) are uncharacterized. This affects hardware redesign for Artemis lunar missions.

**Tractability**: Drop tower or short-radius centrifuge experiments with waste simulant in bench-scale compactor. Measure compaction ratio, compression force, product density. Validate DEM (discrete element method) simulation. Moderate complexity; standard materials testing hardware.

**Scores**:
- **Novelty: 8/10** — No published data on compaction efficiency in partial gravity; critical gap for lunar/Mars system design.
- **Tractability: 7/10** — Requires parabolic flight or drop tower access, or custom centrifuge rig. Simulation validation feasible with open-source DEM software.
- **Relevance: 9/10** — Directly impacts mass and volume of waste containers for lunar missions; safety-critical for mechanical compaction systems.

### Gap 4: Real-Time Sensing and Control of Brine Residual Water Content During In-Container Processing

**Closest Prior Work**: Brine residual containment (BRIC) drying concepts (Ref. 20); Ion exchange and electrodialysis purification (Refs. 21, 22); Brine recovery baseline 85% (Ref. 22)

**Why It Matters**: Brine residual is a viscous, toxic paste that is difficult to monitor and process. Current approaches assume off-line drying or disposal. No in-situ sensor or control strategy exists to monitor water content in real-time during BRIC drying, preventing optimal processing time and safe containerization. Crew cannot verify when residual is "dry enough" for safe storage.

**Tractability**: Develop moisture sensor (e.g., time-domain reflectometry, NIR spectroscopy, or gravimetric monitoring via container mass) and simple control logic (e.g., stop drying at <2% residual moisture). Lab bench test with actual ISS brine simulant or salt mixtures. Could include sensor prototype, proof-of-concept control algorithm.

**Scores**:
- **Novelty: 7/10** — Brine recovery technology exists; real-time sensing and control of residual state is not documented.
- **Tractability: 7/10** — Sensor prototyping and control logic are achievable; requires some materials science (sensor selection) but no advanced manufacturing.
- **Relevance: 7/10** — Improves reliability and crew safety in waste processing; moderate priority if other waste streams are managed first.

### Gap 5: Multi-Stream Synergistic Resource Recovery Modeling and Optimization

**Closest Prior Work**: Trash-to-Gas for methane/water (Ref. 3); Urine distillation + ion exchange (Refs. 15, 22); Food waste composting or thermal degradation (Ref. 30); HMC water recovery (Ref. 5)

**Why It Matters**: Existing literature treats waste streams in isolation (urine → water/gas; food waste → gas; feces → biological inactivation). In practice, mixed waste (food packaging + urine + feces + wipes) enters compactors and processors. No optimization framework exists to allocate mixed waste to multiple concurrent pathways (thermal degradation for gas, distillation for water, ion exchange for salts, compost for feces) to maximize resource yield and minimize residual disposal. This is a systems problem.

**Tractability**: Develop mixed-integer linear or dynamic programming model with waste composition input, pathway efficiencies, and product value/mass trade-offs. Validate with published recovery rates (Refs. 3, 5, 22). Model can be implemented in Python or commercial solver (GUROBI). Feasible for master's or PhD thesis.

**Scores**:
- **Novelty: 8/10** — Resource recovery pathways are known individually; system-level optimization with mixed waste input is not found in literature.
- **Tractability: 6/10** — Requires optimization expertise and access to reliable waste composition and pathway efficiency data. Model validation against flight data is difficult.
- **Relevance: 8/10** — High impact for long-duration missions where every kg of water and kg of waste matters. Informs hardware and operational trade-offs.

### Gap 6: Halophilic and Osmotolerant Microbial Conversion of Brine Residual for Resource Extraction

**Closest Prior Work**: Bioregenerative life support with genetically engineered organisms (Ref. 24); Microbial waste processing in closed loops (Refs. 26, 27); Thermal waste conversion (Ref. 3)

**Why It Matters**: Brine residual (~15% water after ion exchange recovery) is toxic and unsuitable for biological processing via standard organisms. Halophilic and osmotolerant microbes (e.g., *Halomonas*, *Bacillus* spp.) are known to thrive in salt-rich environments and can degrade complex organics. No published work explores bioprocessing of ISS/ECLSS brine residual using such organisms to further extract water, minerals, or energy. This represents unclaimed potential for resource recovery and residual mass reduction.

**Tractability**: Screen existing halophilic isolates for ability to grow on synthetic ISS brine residual simulant (high salt + organic contaminants). Measure growth kinetics, water production, metabolite profile. Lab-scale fermentation feasible; microbiology + analytics only. Risk: regulatory/safety considerations for engineered organisms in ECLSS.

**Scores**:
- **Novelty: 9/10** — Combining halophile bioprocessing with space waste residual is novel; no published work on this interface.
- **Tractability: 6/10** — Microbiology is straightforward, but regulatory approval and testing of engineered organisms in space context is complex. Proof-of-concept is feasible; flight validation is years away.
- **Relevance: 7/10** — Medium-term innovation (5–10 years). Valuable for truly closed-loop missions; less urgent than odor or compaction issues.

## Summary

**Total literature assessed**: 37 real sources (NASA NTRS, ICES proceedings, arXiv, Nature open-access, ResearchGate, NCBI).

**Strongest evidence for gaps**: Quantitative modeling (microbial kinetics, compaction behavior) and real-time process control (brine sensing) are absent or nascent. Waste management is operationally mature (ISS proven) but not predictively characterized for non-1g environments or long-term autonomous missions.

**Highest-impact opportunities**: Microbial dynamics in partial gravity (Gap 1) and odor prediction under compaction (Gap 2) are most directly actionable with existing technology and highest relevance to Artemis/Mars planning.
