## Candidate Research Gaps

### Gap 1: Biofilm Prediction and Adaptive Control Under Mission-Variable Operating Conditions

**Closest Prior Work**: "A Modeling Study on Microbial Growth for the ISS Wastewater Process System: An Update" (NASA 2025, ICES-2025-406); "Potential Biofilm Control Strategies for Extended Spaceflight Missions" (NASA 2023).

**Why It Matters**: ISS operates continuously with stable crew load (~6 people, steady humidity/urine production). Mars transit and lunar surface missions will have variable crew activity (sleep/wake cycles, quarantine isolation, reduced activity during dormancy periods up to 1 year). Current biofilm models assume steady-state or worst-case conditions; they do not predict how biofilm growth/control responds to transient operation. BPA (Biocide-based Protocol Adaptation) could fail if crew activity drops suddenly, allowing rapid biofilm regrowth that biocides cannot suppress. Current mitigation (tank cycling, filter replacement) is empirical; no validated predictive control exists.

**Relevance for Long-Duration/Partial-Gravity**: On Mars surface, crew will have 30-day to multi-year occupancy with extended dormancy (dust storms, equipment maintenance, seasonal temperature swings). Partial gravity systems (unlike microgravity) may allow settling/sedimentation of biofilm, changing control dynamics. Gap: no model connects variable water usage patterns, thermal cycling, gravity environment, and biofilm population dynamics to predict safe biocide dosing and maintenance intervals.

**Tractability**: High. Bench-scale, low-gravity parabolic flight or centrifuge tests could validate biofilm growth models under variable flow/gravity. Microbial culture systems (Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Bacillus subtilis biofilms on ISS materials—already characterized) are hobbyist-tractable. A control-theoretic approach (state-space model + feedback regulator) is within academic scope.

**Novelty/Tractability/Relevance Scoring**:
- **Novelty**: 7/10 — Biofilm modeling exists, but adaptive, mission-profile-dependent prediction + control feedback is novel. ISS data hints at the gap but is limited to steady-state.
- **Tractability**: 8/10 — Hobbyist can culture biofilms on coupon materials, test growth under variable conditions in vitro, and prototype control logic. Parabolic flight test feasible for validation.
- **Relevance**: 9/10 — Critical single point of failure for long-duration missions; biofilm-induced water quality excursion or equipment clogging could necessitate emergency return or habitat abandonment.
- **Overall**: 8/10 (high novelty + relevance + tractability). *Justification*: Addresses known operational challenge with predictive + control contribution; bounded scope; real data available for validation.

### Gap 2: Real-Time Multi-Parameter Water Quality Feedback Control for Autonomous Biocide and pH Adjustment

**Closest Prior Work**: "Real-time quality assurance testing using photonic techniques: Application to iodine water system" (NASA 1993); "52nd International Conference on Environmental Systems ICES-2023-312" (advanced sensor development).

**Why It Matters**: ISS water quality is monitored periodically (grab samples, returned to Earth, analyzed days/weeks later). Crew manually adjust biocide concentration and pH based on ground commands; response time is 1-3 days. On Mars or lunar missions, communication delay (3-20 min one-way) or temporary loss of comms makes manual ground control infeasible. Current ISS approach: fixed biocide dosing schedule (e.g., iodine every 7 days). This is safe but potentially suboptimal—excess iodine increases residual contamination; insufficient dosing allows biofilm escape. Gap: no real-time, on-board feedback control that adjusts biocide/pH based on live total organic carbon (TOC), turbidity, or microbial biomass estimates.

**Relevance for Long-Duration/Partial-Gravity**: Autonomous, closed-loop control is mandatory for missions >30 days without ground contact. Partial gravity may change water chemistry equilibria (pH buffering, mineral precipitation, biofilm metabolism rates), so pre-set dosing schedules from ISS cannot directly transfer.

**Tractability**: Medium-to-High. Sensors (UV-Vis spectrometry for TOC, colorimetric pH, turbidity sensors, emerging planar conductivity probes) are commercial, low-mass products. Control algorithms (proportional-integral feedback, adaptive thresholds) are well-established. Challenge: integrating sensor and actuation hardware into a microgravity-compatible WPA module; validating sensor accuracy in microbially-challenged water over 12+ months. Hobbyist-level modeling and preliminary testing feasible; flight-qualifiable prototype (~2-3 year project).

**Novelty/Tractability/Relevance Scoring**:
- **Novelty**: 8/10 — Autonomous feedback control for spacecraft water is emerging (arXiv, recent ICES papers) but not fielded. Multi-parameter integration is novel.
- **Tractability**: 7/10 — Sensors and control software are mature. Challenge is hardware integration, microgravity sensor calibration stability, and long-term reliability. Bench test and ISS test-bed experiment realistic within ~2 years.
- **Relevance**: 9/10 — Mandatory capability for Mars missions; eliminates dependency on ground control. Improves ISS operations efficiency immediately (reduced over-biociding).
- **Overall**: 8/10 (high novelty + relevance; medium-high tractability). *Justification*: Addresses operationally critical gap; leverages available technology; bounded R&D scope; demonstration on ISS is credible path to validation.

### Gap 3: Unified Thermal and Hydraulic Modeling of Vapor Compression Distillation Under Variable Microgravity and Transient Duty Cycles

**Closest Prior Work**: "ISS Water Recovery System, Vapor Compression Distillation" (NASA 2019); "Review of vapor compression refrigeration in microgravity" (ICES-2020); "Vapor Compression and Thermoelectric Heat Pumps" (NASA 2014).

**Why It Matters**: ISS WRS UPA operates at steady state (continuous urine feed ~1.5 L/person/day). Ground tests and design simulations use lumped-parameter models or commercial heat-pump cycle solvers (CoolProp, REFPROP) with gravity-averaged correlations for heat transfer and two-phase flow. These models do not capture: (1) transient cold-start behavior when UPA is powered on after dormancy, (2) microgravity-specific flow regimes (slug flow, annular flow, annular-slug transition) that conventional correlations miss, (3) variable evaporator/condenser performance during transients, (4) compressor surge/overflow during off-nominal operation. Current practice: conservative design margins (oversized hardware) and empirical tuning on-orbit. Gap: no high-fidelity, microgravity-aware VCD model that predicts performance over full mission envelope (startup, nominal, degraded, emergency shutdown-restart).

**Relevance for Long-Duration/Partial-Gravity**: Mars mission water recovery must tolerate dormancy (up to 1 year) followed by restart under Martian gravity (~0.38g). Thermal model must predict whether condensing coefficient, evaporator heat load, and compressor efficiency change due to gravity. Partial gravity systems may operate at higher flow rates or pressure drops where microgravity assumptions break down. Novel thermal designs (e.g., falling-film evaporator, capillary-driven condenser) for partial gravity are unexplored; scaling theory is absent.

**Tractability**: Medium. Requires computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modeling of two-phase flow in microgravity (using Volume-of-Fluid or Level-Set methods) coupled to system-level thermodynamic solver. Parabolic flight experiments to measure heat transfer, pressure drop, and flow regime maps for relevant fluids (water, organic solvents) are feasible but require specialized facility access. Data reduction and model validation can be done post-flight by students/researchers. Full model development: 2-3 year academic project.

**Novelty/Tractability/Relevance Scoring**:
- **Novelty**: 8/10 — Microgravity multiphase VCD modeling is sparse; most NASA work is proprietary or lumped-parameter. Transient, partial-gravity extension is novel.
- **Tractability**: 6/10 — Requires CFD expertise and parabolic flight access (not hobby-level). Bench-scale flow loop tests and numerical studies are feasible. Actual ISS experiment would validate but is expensive/rare.
- **Relevance**: 8/10 — Direct impact on UPA efficiency, power consumption, and reliability predictions for Mars. Supports novel partial-gravity hardware designs.
- **Overall**: 7/10 (high novelty + relevance; medium tractability). *Justification*: Addresses performance prediction gap for critical hardware. Requires specialized facilities but contributions are high-impact for exploration missions.

### Gap 4: Integrated Multi-Stream Water Recovery (Urine + Fecal + Food Waste) with Distributed On-Demand Polishing

**Closest Prior Work**: "Environmental Control and Life Support (ECLS) System Options for Mars Transit and Mars Surface Missions" (NASA 2023); "A Review of Baseline Assumptions and Ersatz Waste Streams for Partial Gravity Habitats" (NASA 2022); "Humidity Condensate Treatment using a Hybrid Life Support System" (ICES-2025-142).

**Why It Matters**: ISS WRS treats urine (UPA + WPA) and humidity condensate separately. Food waste, fecal matter, and hygiene greywater are not currently recycled (ISS uses consumable water for hygiene; waste is jettisoned or stored). For Mars missions (1100+ days), integrated treatment of all water sources is mandatory to achieve 98% recovery target. Current water balance for Mars shows: urine ~1.5 L/person/day, humidity condensate ~1.0 L/person/day, fecal leachate ~0.5 L/person/day, hygiene greywater ~1.5 L/person/day (if recycled) = ~5 L/person/day to recover. Gap: no integrated, modular water recovery system that handles all streams with adaptive polishing (e.g., TOC or microbial loading triggers multi-stage filtration, UV, or bioreactor stages on-demand rather than continuously).

**Relevance for Long-Duration/Partial-Gravity**: Gravity simplifies solid-liquid separation (settling, centrifugation) compared to microgravity. Fecal and food waste can use gravity to concentrate solids and separate liquids in partial gravity, reducing electrical/mechanical processing. Modular, on-demand polishing allows staged treatment: primary separation (gravity settler in partial gravity), secondary biological treatment (rotating bioreactor), tertiary polishing (RO or advanced oxidation) only when TOC exceeds threshold. This reduces power, consumables, and complexity vs. continuous treatment.

**Tractability**: Medium-High. Subsystems (urine UPA, humidity bioreactor, gravity settler) exist separately. Integration challenge is process control and interface compatibility (e.g., bioreactor effluent salinity compatible with WPA feed). Bench-scale test of integrated pilot (2-3 person equivalent) feasible in terrestrial gravity; parabolic flight trial of partial-gravity settler feasible. Model development (component balances, feedback logic) is hobbyist-tractable; hardware fabrication and testing requires lab facilities (~1-2 year student project).

**Novelty/Tractability/Relevance Scoring**:
- **Novelty**: 9/10 — Integrated multi-source water recovery is conceptually new for human spaceflight. Existing work treats streams independently. On-demand polishing logic is novel.
- **Tractability**: 7/10 — Combines known technologies (UPA, bioreactors, RO, settlers, sensors, control logic). Bench test and model validation feasible; hardware integration straightforward with modest engineering.
- **Relevance**: 9/10 — Essential for Mars water closure; enables crew hygiene and reduces consumable burden. High public/mission interest.
- **Overall**: 8/10 (very high novelty + relevance; medium tractability). *Justification*: Addresses known mission-level requirement; leverages existing components; validation path clear (terrestrial bench, parabolic flight, ISS testbed). Contribution is systems engineering + control, not fundamental science.

### Gap 5: Predictive Modeling of Mineral Scale/Precipitation in Variable Gravity and Chemistry Regimes (Extended Mission Scenario)

**Closest Prior Work**: "Water Recovery from Brines to Further Close the Water Recovery Loop" (NASA 2014); "Design Status of the Capillary Brine Residual in Containment Water Recovery System" (NASA 2015); various references to mineral precipitation in UPA.

**Why It Matters**: ISS UPA limits water recovery to ~87% to prevent urea precipitation and hardness mineral formation (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, PO₄³⁻ salts) that would clog or corrode hardware. Downstream brine processor assembly recovers additional 10-11% from the brine. On Mars surface under variable thermal cycling (average -63°C day/night swings) and with regolith-derived makeup water (likely high in Ca²⁺, SO₄²⁻, Cl⁻ from salt deposits), water chemistry will drift. Current ISS design assumes Earth-sourced water in (from resupply); Mars missions must recycle all water indefinitely, and makeup water (from melted subsurface ice or regolith extraction) is unknown in composition. Gap: no coupled geochemical-thermal model that predicts precipitation behavior as concentration ratio, pH, and temperature vary over extended duty cycles. Precipitation kinetics are sensitive to nucleation sites (biofilm, corrosion products, dust), which are mission-dependent.

**Relevance for Long-Duration/Partial-Gravity**: Thermal cycling in partial gravity (e.g., lunar day/night) affects diffusion and settling of micro-precipitates. In microgravity, any settled mineral must be removed mechanistically (filter, centrifuge). In partial gravity, gravity can assist; but unpredicted precipitation could still clog fine filters or catalyze corrosion. Regolith-based makeup water introduces new salts not seen in ISS operations; scaling models are needed.

**Tractability**: Medium. Requires geochemical equilibrium models (Phreeqc, EQ3/6, or custom) coupled to mission water balance and thermal model. Bench test: prepare synthetic Mars regolith water, recycle it through mock WPA/brine processor, measure precipitation over 100+ cycles, validate model predictions. Achievable in lab with no special gravity facilities. Model development and validation: 1.5-2 year thesis project.

**Novelty/Tractability/Relevance Scoring**:
- **Novelty**: 7/10 — Mineral scaling is well-studied in terrestrial water treatment; application to Mars water recovery + thermal cycling + gravity effects is novel but incremental.
- **Tractability**: 8/10 — Geochemical tools are mature and open-source. Bench test is straightforward. Does not require microgravity or advanced facilities.
- **Relevance**: 7/10 — Important for long-duration Mars missions to avoid hardware failure; not immediately critical for ISS or lunar 30-day sortie.
- **Overall**: 7/10 (good novelty + tractability + moderate relevance). *Justification*: Addresses design-life risk for Mars; leverages available modeling tools; lab validation feasible; incremental advance vs. novel breakthrough.

### Gap 6: Microbial Biomass Quantification via In-Situ, Non-Destructive Optical Biomarkers (Real-Time Proxy for Biofilm Burden)

**Closest Prior Work**: "Development of a high-resolution multiplex qPCR method to profile microbial consortia in spaceflight water recovery systems" (recent NCBI/bioRxiv preprint); "52nd ICES-2023-110" (microbial detection sensors); "Real-time quality assurance testing using photonic techniques" (NASA 1993).

**Why It Matters**: Current biomass monitoring on ISS relies on: (1) periodic grab samples (analyzed on ground weeks later for microbial ATP, heterotrophic plate count, qPCR—expensive, delayed), or (2) surrogate metrics like total organic carbon (TOC) or biocide concentration. TOC is slow (~5 min response) and cannot distinguish microbial biomass from organic contaminants (urea metabolites, glycerol from antifreeze, etc.). No real-time on-board sensor directly quantifies viable biomass or biofilm burden without destructive sampling. Gap: optical biomarkers (intrinsic fluorescence of microbial pigments, autofluorescence of NADH, light scattering from cell aggregates, or fluorogenic enzyme substrates) could provide real-time biomass proxy for control feedback, but none are validated for ISS water conditions and microbially-diverse consortia.

**Relevance for Long-Duration/Partial-Gravity**: Autonomous control of biocide dosing (Gap 2) requires real-time biomass feedback. Partial-gravity environments may support different microbial community structure (e.g., more filamentous fungi/actinomycetes that flocculate vs. planktonic pseudomonads in microgravity), changing optical signatures. No Earth-gravity data on these microbial shifts exist.

**Tractability**: High. Optical sensors (fluorescence, turbidity, light scattering) are commercial, low-mass, low-power, and hobbyist-accessible. Validation requires culturing ISS-derived water microbes (or purchasing Pseudomonas aeruginosa strains) and correlating optical signals to standard counts (viable plate, qPCR). Bench test feasible in 6-12 months. Development of calibration model (machine learning: regression of optical signatures to biomass) is low-barrier.

**Novelty/Tractability/Relevance Scoring**:
- **Novelty**: 8/10 — Application of optical biomarkers to spacecraft water is emerging; multimodal sensor fusion is novel. Individual techniques (fluorescence, scatter) are known.
- **Tractability**: 9/10 — Sensors, culturing, and ML model development are hobbyist-level or better. No specialized facilities required beyond a biosafety cabinet and spectrophotometer.
- **Relevance**: 8/10 — Enables real-time feedback control (Gap 2); immediate benefit for ISS and future missions. High priority for NASA.
- **Overall**: 8/10 (very high tractability + novelty + relevance). *Justification*: Low barrier to entry; direct path to ISS demonstration; leverage emerging sensor tech; high impact on autonomous water management.
