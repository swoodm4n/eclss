## Candidate Research Gaps

### Gap 1: Predictive Modeling of Flame Spread Rate in Partial-Gravity Environments

**Name:** Real-time flame spread prediction and containment for partial-gravity habitats

**Closest Prior Work:** 
- Topical reviews by Liao & Gollner (2023) identify partial-gravity combustion as critical gap
- FM2 lunar mission (late 2026) will provide first on-surface data (minutes vs. seconds from parabolic flights)
- Saffire I-VI provided microgravity data, but no sustained partial-gravity testing facility yet

**Why It Matters:** 
Lunar (0.16 g) and Mars (0.38 g) habitats will operate at elevated O2 (26-34%) and reduced pressure (56-73 kPa). Current evidence suggests flames can be sustained in partial gravity when extinction occurs at micro- and normal gravity. Without predictive models, crew emergency response and habitat design margins are speculative.

**Tractability & Validation:**
Moderate-high tractability. Validation could use:
- ZGRF (NASA centrifuge at WSTF) for sustained partial-gravity combustion tests
- Bench-top reduced-pressure chamber with variable gravity simulation
- FM2 post-flight data integration (2027 onward)
- Scaling laws from micro/normal gravity data with partial-G perturbation modeling

Hobbyist validation difficult but feasible at research-lab scale (thermal camera, controlled fuel geometry, variable-pressure chamber).

**Novelty / Tractability / Relevance Scoring:**
- Novelty: 8/10 (Few published partial-gravity flame spread models; FM2 data not yet public; interpolation methods underdeveloped)
- Tractability: 6/10 (Requires centrifuge or parabolic flights for full validation; simplified model feasible; high-fidelity CFD coupling challenging)
- Relevance: 9/10 (Critical for Moon/Mars habitat design; affects crew egress procedures, suppression agent sizing, material selection; no other research addresses this directly for long-duration ops)
- **Overall:** 7.7/10 – Strong mission relevance, medium model tractability, high novelty; FM2 data will break bottleneck in 2027.

### Gap 2: Cool-Flame Detection and Characterization for Spacecraft Fire Sensing

**Name:** Sensor design and calibration for cool-flame identification in low-oxygen and post-suppression environments

**Closest Prior Work:**
- Glenn Center studies (2020s) identified cool flames in ISS experiments—fuel producing CO/formaldehyde after visible flame extinction
- No published detection method for cool flames in spacecraft sensors
- Current ISS fire detection uses visual/smoke/thermal sensors (blank for low-temperature combustion)

**Why It Matters:**
Cool flames are a combustion mode in microgravity at low oxygen where visible flames extinguish but fuel continues to burn. Crew would not see or sense this with standard detectors, creating a "smoldering" mode fire risk. Duration and re-ignition potential unknown. Critical for unattended spacecraft and long-duration missions where crew may enter a compartment minutes after a suppression event.

**Tractability & Validation:**
Medium tractability. Cool-flame detection requires:
- Develop low-temperature infrared (LWIR) or CO/CO2 spectral signatures in lab
- Microfluidic or small-scale chamber experiments at microgravity conditions
- Ground validation: parabolic flights or drop towers with spectrometry
- Integration into spacecraft cabin sensors (chemical sensor miniaturization)

Validation scalable to bench with FTIR spectrometer and drop-tower time.

**Novelty / Tractability / Relevance Scoring:**
- Novelty: 9/10 (Cool flames documented but no sensor solution published; largely unexplored for spacecraft)
- Tractability: 5/10 (Requires microgravity testing and spectral method development; sensor integration complex; limited parabolic flight opportunity)
- Relevance: 8/10 (Hidden fire risk; affects crew safety protocols; essential for extended-duration missions; lower relevance for near-term ISS but critical for exploration)
- **Overall:** 7.3/10 – Very high novelty, medium tractability, high relevance; experimental path clear but requires resources.

### Gap 3: Multi-Contaminant Post-Fire Atmosphere Recovery and Sensor Validation

**Name:** Optimization of trace-contaminant removal for mixed combustion byproducts in long-duration missions

**Closest Prior Work:**
- CCT-ARS includes post-fire recovery module (NASA tech brief, 2010s-2020s)
- Current ISS air revitalization assumes rapid crew evacuation and system restart
- No published data on multi-hour contamination profiles from full-scale spacecraft fires with complex material mixes
- Saffire-IV-VI provided smoke/aerosol composition in Cygnus but limited trace gas data

**Why It Matters:**
Post-fire atmosphere recovery is listed as one component of current life-support systems but under-validated for realistic scenarios. Long-duration deep-space missions (2+ years to Mars) cannot rely on evacuation or re-supply. If fire occurs with material mixes (fabric, composites, electronics, coolant), trace contaminants (CO, aldehydes, halogenated compounds, particulates) may exceed air-revitalization capacity or saturate filters. Current specs assume <24-hour contamination; longer fires or pre-contaminated baseline uncertain.

**Tractability & Validation:**
Medium-high tractability. Requires:
- Instrumented large-scale fire tests (Saffire-style) with material-mix matrix (textiles, epoxy, aluminum, polyurethane, etc.)
- Trace-gas chromatography and particulate sizing during and post-fire
- Chamber-scale air-revitalization hardware tests with captured exhaust
- Crew exposure time/dose models using ECLSS flow and contaminant data
- Bench validation: small fires in sealed chamber, monitor removal rates with sensors

Scalable to research-lab level.

**Novelty / Tractability / Relevance Scoring:**
- Novelty: 7/10 (Post-fire recovery known as requirement, but integrated validation lacking; no multi-material/multi-hour scenario data published)
- Tractability: 7/10 (Builds on Saffire infrastructure; sensor/sampling well-established; long-duration tests feasible; modeling of removal rates standard ECLSS engineering)
- Relevance: 8/10 (Critical for crew safety in deep-space missions; affects emergency procedures, air-revit sizing, mission timeline; moderate relevance near-term ISS, high for exploration)
- **Overall:** 7.3/10 – Medium-high novelty, good tractability, high relevance; clear experimental path; enables ECLSS design confidence for long-duration.

### Gap 4: Fire Suppression Agent Effectiveness Across Gravity Regimes

**Name:** Development and validation of interpolation models for suppression agent efficacy in partial-gravity environments

**Closest Prior Work:**
- Water mist and CO2 tested extensively in normal and microgravity (NIST, NASA, ESA)
- Water mist found more effective than CO2 on mass basis in microgravity
- No suppression tests in partial gravity (0.1–0.4 g range)
- FM2 lunar mission will not test suppression agents (only combustion)

**Why It Matters:**
Lunar/Mars habitats will need suppression agents. Water mist is candidate but efficacy unknown at 0.16 g (Moon) or 0.38 g (Mars). Droplet dynamics, heat-transfer coefficients, and flame extinction mechanisms change with gravity. If habitat design assumes microgravity-tested agent performance but actual partial-gravity behavior differs, suppression failure becomes mission risk. Current approach: use conservative margins, but data-driven model would improve reliability and reduce agent mass budget.

**Tractability & Validation:**
Medium-low tractability. Requires:
- Centrifuge (ZGRF or similar) with large enough test cell for flame + agent injection
- High-speed imaging and thermocouple arrays at multiple gravity levels
- Multiple suppression agents (water mist primary, CO2 backup, alternatives)
- Flame extinction criteria and scaling laws
- Ground validation: drop-tower drops are seconds; centrifuge sustained tests minutes

Centrifuge time is limiting resource (expensive, limited availability).

**Novelty / Tractability / Relevance Scoring:**
- Novelty: 8/10 (No published suppression agent data in partial gravity; interpolation methods not attempted; high-value gap for design engineers)
- Tractability: 4/10 (Requires centrifuge facility; high cost; limited flight hardware options; scaling laws complex; few groups have necessary capability)
- Relevance: 8/10 (Direct impact on lunar/Mars habitat design; affects crew safety and mission go/no-go; low relevance for ISS, critical for exploration)
- **Overall:** 6.7/10 – High novelty and relevance, low-medium tractability; resource-limited bottleneck; strong motivator for agency funding.

### Gap 5: Real-Time Smoke and Particulate Distribution Mapping in Variable-Gravity Cabins

**Name:** 3D smoke-spread modeling and sensor network design for partial-gravity spacecraft geometry

**Closest Prior Work:**
- ISS smoke detection uses ventilation-system sampling (1D near ducts)
- Saffire IV-VI measured smoke aerosol composition but in small flow chamber
- NASA ECLSS models advection-diffusion in normal gravity; microgravity models simplified (no buoyancy)
- No published 3D models for partial-gravity smoke transport in realistic cabin geometries

**Why It Matters:**
In microgravity, smoke doesn't rise—it follows ventilation and diffusion. In partial gravity, smoke behavior is intermediate: some settling, but not Earth-like plume rise. Crew egress decisions and firefighter routing depend on where smoke accumulates. Current sensor placement (ISS: ventilation ducts only) works for microgravity but may miss pockets in partial-gravity habitats. Larger cabins (lunar bases, Mars hab, transit spacecraft) require multi-zone detection. Model uncertainty affects crew emergency procedures and sensor system design.

**Tractability & Validation:**
Medium tractability. Requires:
- CFD model coupling gravity term with multi-phase smoke (liquid aerosol + gas)
- Validation: parabolic flights or centrifuge with particle tracers (PIV) and multiple sensors
- Bench validation: reduced-pressure chamber with variable-speed circulation fan (mock partial-G diffusion)
- Sensor placement optimization via simulation

Moderate computational and experimental effort.

**Novelty / Tractability / Relevance Scoring:**
- Novelty: 7/10 (3D ECLSS modeling exists for other contaminants; gravity-dependent smoke transport not published; sensor optimization for partial-G not addressed)
- Tractability: 6/10 (CFD tractable; validation requires facility time; multi-phase modeling adds complexity; applicable to ISS, achievable in research context)
- Relevance: 7/10 (Affects crew safety, emergency response, sensor design; moderate relevance for ISS (microgravity model sufficient), high for exploration missions)
- **Overall:** 6.7/10 – Medium novelty, medium tractability, medium-high relevance; enables design of next-generation fire-detection networks.

### Gap 6: Material Flammability Envelope for Habitat Operating Conditions (Elevated O₂, Reduced P, Partial G)

**Name:** Experimental data and scaling laws for material flammability under combined environmental stressors (O₂ %, pressure, gravity)

**Closest Prior Work:**
- NASA STD-3001 and earlier standards list material flammability limits (typically 21% O2, 1 atm, normal G)
- Saffire I-III tested materials but primarily aimed at flame spread, not flammability threshold
- NIST/NASA studies show flammability limits change with pressure and gravity independently
- Future habitat designs (Lunar: 34% O2, 56.5 kPa; Mars: ~35% O2, ~60 kPa) require new data
- Current practice: conservative deration; no validated combined-factor model

**Why It Matters:**
Materials deemed "non-flammable" on Earth may ignite in lunar/Mars habitats due to combined effects: higher O2, lower pressure (slows quenching, lowers extinction limits), and partial gravity (slows buoyant extinction). Current material selection relies on single-factor testing or intuition. Designing habitat material lists for exploration requires predictive model to avoid catastrophic surprises. Over-conservative material restrictions waste mass and cost; under-conservative risks crew.

**Tractability & Validation:**
Medium-high tractability. Requires:
- Systematic ignition-limit testing across matrix: O2 (15–35%), pressure (40–100 kPa), gravity (0.1–1 g)
- Parabolic flights (variable G) + pressure chamber + oxygen enrichment
- Sample matrix: representative textiles, foams, composites, elastomers
- Empirical fit or semi-empirical model to interpolate across conditions
- Bench validation: small-scale ignition rig in reduced-pressure + variable-O2 chamber (no partial G needed for initial model)

Expensive but well-scoped; many NASA/contractor labs have needed equipment.

**Novelty / Tractability / Relevance Scoring:**
- Novelty: 8/10 (No published combined-factor flammability envelope for exploration habitats; scattered single-factor data; critical gap for design)
- Tractability: 7/10 (Requires facility access and many test runs; methodology standard; scaling laws plausible; bench validation feasible without centrifuge)
- Relevance: 9/10 (Direct impact on habitat design, EVA suit materials, emergency equipment; affects mission safety and mass budget; top priority for exploration programs)
- **Overall:** 8/10 – High novelty, good tractability, very high relevance; strong candidate for near-term investment; foundational for habitat design.
